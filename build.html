BravePay · decision note

Build or buy the core

What it would actually take to build our own core banking system instead of renting one — scope, team, time, cost, and the parts no budget can shorten.

3 September 2026 · prepared in response to the question directly

Recommendation

Buy the core. Keep our own complete copy of our financial history so that leaving later stays possible — and revisit the question at around 100,000 active accounts.

01

The headline number

Roughly 300–400 person-months of engineering, 24–36 months elapsed, a team of 11–13 — and that is only about 40% of the job.

The software is the part everyone estimates. Certification, the compliance regime, and operational track record are what actually decide whether you can go live, and none of them compress no matter how good the engineers are.

This note takes the question seriously rather than dismissing it. The scope below is taken from BankOne's own published feature list — seventeen functional areas — so the comparison is like for like.

That last row is the good news: our app, admin console and API layer already exist. We are not starting from nothing. But roughly 80% of the effort sits in the top four rows, and we have none of it today.

Why the ledger is the hard one

It must never lose a transaction, must close cleanly every single night without exception, and must be able to reconstruct any historical position on demand years later. It is not a hard algorithm — it is a system where a rare bug is a financial loss and an audit finding rather than a support ticket. That changes how it has to be built, tested and staffed.

03

The team

Not generalists. This needs people who have built a ledger before, and they are scarce and expensive in every market.

  • 2Core ledger engineers Senior. The hardest hire on this list, by some distance.
  • 2Product and lending engineers Interest accrual and restructuring are where correctness bugs hide.
  • 1Regulatory reporting engineer Unglamorous, permanent, and the formats keep changing.
  • 1Payments integration engineer Owns certification with the national payment system.
  • 1Cards engineer Brings a card compliance regime with them.
  • 2QA engineers Financial correctness testing, not click-testing.
  • 1Infrastructure and reliability The batch has to run every night, forever.
  • 1Security engineer Continuous, not a pre-launch review.
  • 1Banking business analyst Someone who has actually filed a regulatory return.
  • 1Engineering lead Architecture and the regulator-facing conversations.

The role most often skipped, and most often fatal

The banking analyst. Without someone who has worked inside a microfinance bank and filed its returns, engineers will build the wrong thing confidently and only discover it at examination. This is not a role to backfill later.

04

How the time goes

Anyone quoting twelve months is quoting the happy path for the software and quietly ignoring certification.

05

The comparison is not close

Against all of that, BankOne's published pricing has been reported in the region of $1,500–2,500 to implement and roughly $30 per user per month, tapering as user numbers rise — figures that are dated and must be re-quoted before anyone relies on them.

Even at ten times that, it is not close — and the gap widens once the recurring costs above are counted.

06

Four things money cannot compress

  1. Examiner confidence. A regulator's examiner has seen the major platforms at hundreds of institutions. They have never seen ours. Every examination becomes an argument about our ledger instead of a conversation about our business.
  2. Track record. "Our nightly close has run cleanly for four years" is something that can only be earned by waiting four years.
  3. Certification. Connecting to the national payment system is a queue and a process. Money does not move it.
  4. Operational maturity. Someone has to be awake at two in the morning when the batch fails, and know what to do.
07

The argument that actually decides it

Nobody has ever chosen a bank because its general ledger was built in-house.

Twelve engineers on core banking for three years is twelve engineers not building invoicing, payroll, expense tracking and the customer experience — the only parts of BravePay that a customer weighs against an alternative.

That is the real cost, and it does not appear on any budget line. The build is not just expensive; it is expensive in exactly the resource that produces our differentiation.

08

The narrower question worth asking

"Should we build a core banking system" is almost always no. A better question is: should we own the ledger and rent the rails?

That is a genuine option — roughly 30 to 40% of the scope. Our own double-entry ledger and product engine, with payments, cards and regulatory reporting still bought in. Some lenders do exactly this, deliberately.

Still no, for one specific reason

The ledger is precisely the component a regulator scrutinises hardest, and we are a deposit-taking institution. A lender not holding customer deposits faces a different calculation and might reasonably build. Holding deposits is what makes the home-made ledger the expensive choice.

09

When building does make sense

Three conditions. You would want all three before reopening this.

  1. Scale where per-user vendor fees exceed the cost of a build team — hundreds of thousands of accounts, not thousands.
  2. The vendor is demonstrably blocking products we cannot ship any other way.
  3. We intend to sell core banking to other institutions — becoming a platform rather than a customer of one.

None are true today. The second could become true, which is why the exit path matters more than the decision itself.

10

What to do instead

Buy the core, and keep the door open

Proceed with a vendor core, and treat building as a design constraint rather than a plan. Concretely: keep our own shadow ledger and our own record of every customer instruction, so that at any moment we hold a complete copy of our own financial history.

That is what makes leaving possible later without a hostage negotiation. The cost is small, it improves reconciliation and support regardless, and it is already in the integration plan.

Revisit at roughly 100,000 active accounts, or the first time the vendor blocks a product we are certain about. Until then this is a solved question, and reopening it costs more than it returns.
